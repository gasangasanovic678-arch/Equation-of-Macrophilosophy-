\begin{abstract}
We reconstruct the macro-philosophical index $\Ph$ as a local Leibnizian \emph{calculus ratiocinator}. The representation theorem characterizes the weighted geometric mean as the unique aggregator on $[0,1]^n$ satisfying five independent, non-circular axioms. The coordinate basis is not stipulated: we parametrize the class of schools by a product space $\Th=\Th_O\times\Th_E\times\Th_S\times\Th_R\times\Th_C\times\Th_\chi\times\Th_a$, define each coordinate as a function of its own parameter only, and prove \emph{functional independence} of the seven coordinates (Theorem~\ref{thm:indep}); a separate difference-making theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:nonred}) records the weaker property that survives without the parametrization. We prove a \emph{dimension theorem} (Theorem~\ref{thm:dim}): seven is the minimal number of coordinates needed to discriminate the class of schools, so the basis is complete as well as independent. Coherence is defined on the cyclic core of the dependency graph, proved invariant under cycle-irrelevant extensions and cycle-neutral edge additions, proved invariant under graph isomorphism, and proved monotone under edge deletion. The weights are identified up to a single linear constraint, and calibration is reduced to convex optimization. A worked comparison of three schools illustrates the calculus. Every claim in the abstract is a numbered theorem, proposition, or corollary in the body.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction: the Leibnizian programme}\label{sec:intro}

Leibniz's programme has two components. The \emph{characteristica universalis} is a language in which every substantive assertion can be expressed without ambiguity. The \emph{calculus ratiocinator} is a procedure that decides disputes by computation: \emph{Calculemus}. A representation theorem for an index of philosophical theories realises the second component locally: it converts a dispute about the \emph{form} of the index into a theorem, leaving disagreement localised in the inputs. The first component is realised locally by a \emph{derivation of the coordinate basis} that is both complete (Theorem~\ref{thm:dim}) and functionally independent (Theorem~\ref{thm:indep}).

The present note does six things. Section~\ref{sec:coord} parametrizes the class of schools, defines the seven coordinates, and proves functional independence, difference-making, and dimension-minimality. Section~\ref{sec:rep} gives the representation theorem and verifies independence of the axioms. Section~\ref{sec:coh} defines coherence on the cyclic core; Section~\ref{sec:inv} proves the four invariance/monotonicity results. Section~\ref{sec:prop} collects properties of the index, including threshold dominance. Section~\ref{sec:comp} states the computational problem, its NP-hardness, and its APX-hardness on the cyclic core. Section~\ref{sec:calib} treats the weights: identification and convex calibration. Section~\ref{sec:leibniz} states in what sense the result is Leibnizian. Section~\ref{sec:case} works a three-school comparison. Section~\ref{sec:disc} separates what is settled from what is not.

\section{The coordinate basis: parametrization, independence, minimality}\label{sec:coord}

\subsection{Schools and their parameter space}

\begin{definition}[School]\label{def:school}
A \emph{school} is a triple $S=(T_S,\mathcal{L}_S,\preceq_S)$ where $T_S$ is a finite set of theses, $\mathcal{L}_S$ is the language in which the theses are expressed, and $\preceq_S$ is a justification preorder on $T_S$. The dependency graph $D(S)$ is the directed graph of the strict part of $\preceq_S$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[The four constitutive questions]\label{def:questions}
Every school must answer, implicitly or explicitly:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(Q1)] \emph{What is?} --- the ontological question: what does the school admit as existent?
\item[(Q2)] \emph{How is it known?} --- the epistemological question: by what faculty or procedure are theses justified?
\item[(Q3)] \emph{How is it expressed?} --- the semantic question: what is the expressive power of $\mathcal{L}_S$ and what does it leave unsaid?
\item[(Q4)] \emph{How does it stand to other schools?} --- the relational question: what is the school's position in the field?
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Parameter space]\label{def:param}
Let $\Th_O,\Th_E,\Th_S,\Th_R,\Th_C$ be nonempty sets of \emph{answers} to (Q1)--(Q3) and to the Leibnizian condition (see below), and let $\Th_\chi$ be a set of \emph{consistency regimes} (classical, paraconsistent, graded, \dots). Let $\Th_a$ be the set of finite directed graphs. The \emph{parameter space} is
\[
\Th \;=\; \Th_O\times\Th_E\times\Th_S\times\Th_R\times\Th_C\times\Th_\chi\times\Th_a .
\]
A \emph{parametrized school} is an element $\theta=(\theta_O,\theta_E,\theta_S,\theta_R,\theta_C,\theta_\chi,\theta_a)\in\Th$, together with a realization map $\rho:\Th\to\{\text{schools}\}$ that assigns to $\theta$ a school whose answers to (Q1)--(Q3) are $\theta_O,\theta_E,\theta_S$, whose language is $\theta_C$-formalizable, whose consistency regime is $\theta_\chi$, and whose dependency graph is $\theta_a$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
The parameter space is a product by construction. This is the structural fact that will make functional independence a theorem rather than a list of examples. The price is that we must justify the product structure: this is done by Proposition~\ref{prop:complete} and Theorem~\ref{thm:dim} below.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Coordinate scores]\label{def:scores}
For $i\in\{O,E,S,R,C\}$, let $\sigma_i:\Th_i\to[0,1]$ be a \emph{score function} on the $i$-th factor. Define the $i$-th coordinate of $\theta\in\Th$ by
\[
s_i(\theta) \;=\; \sigma_i(\theta_i).
\]
For $\chi$, let $\sigma_\chi:\Th_\chi\to[0,1]$ be a consistency score, and for $a$, let $\sigma_a:\Th_a\to[0,1]$ be the coherence score of Section~\ref{sec:coh}. Define $\chi(\theta)=\sigma_\chi(\theta_\chi)$ and $a(\theta)=\sigma_a(\theta_a)$.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Completeness of the basis}

\begin{proposition}[Discriminating adequacy]\label{prop:complete}
Let $\theta,\theta'\in\Th$. If $\theta_i=\theta_i'$ for all $i\in\{O,E,S,R,C,\chi,a\}$, then $\rho(\theta)$ and $\rho(\theta')$ are the same school. Consequently the seven coordinates separate points of $\Th$ up to realization.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Agreement on $\theta_O,\theta_E,\theta_S$ fixes the answers to (Q1)--(Q3); agreement on $\theta_C$ fixes the formalizability class of the language; agreement on $\theta_\chi$ fixes the consistency regime; agreement on $\theta_a$ fixes the dependency graph. By Definition~\ref{def:param}, $\rho(\theta)$ and $\rho(\theta')$ then agree on all components of Definition~\ref{def:school} and on the Leibnizian condition, hence are the same school up to the realization map.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Functional independence}

\begin{theorem}[Functional independence of the coordinate basis]\label{thm:indep}
For each $i\in\{O,E,S,R,C,\chi,a\}$ there is no function $f_i$ of the other six coordinates such that $s_i=f_i\bigl((s_j)_{j\ne i}\bigr)$ on $\Th$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix $i$ and suppose for contradiction that such $f_i$ exists. Choose $\theta,\theta'\in\Th$ with $\theta_i\ne\theta_i'$ but $\theta_j=\theta_j'$ for all $j\ne i$; such a pair exists because $\Th$ is a product and each factor is nonempty. Then $s_j(\theta)=s_j(\theta')$ for $j\ne i$ (by Definition~\ref{def:scores}, since $s_j$ depends only on $\theta_j$), so $f_i$ applied to the common values gives $s_i(\theta)=s_i(\theta')$. But $\theta_i\ne\theta_i'$ and $\sigma_i$ is injective on $\Th_i$ (we may choose $\Th_i$ and $\sigma_i$ so that this holds; see Remark~\ref{rem:injective}), so $s_i(\theta)\ne s_i(\theta')$, a contradiction.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Injectivity of $\sigma_i$]\label{rem:injective}
The proof requires $\sigma_i$ to be injective on $\Th_i$. This is a modelling choice: we may take $\Th_i\subseteq[0,1]$ and $\sigma_i=\mathrm{id}$, in which case injectivity is automatic. The substantive claim of Theorem~\ref{thm:indep} is that the \emph{product structure} of $\Th$ is what enforces independence; once the structure is granted, injectivity is a trivial normalization.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Difference-making (the weaker property that survives without the parametrization)}

\begin{theorem}[Difference-making]\label{thm:nonred}
For each coordinate $c\in\{\sO,\sE,\sS,\sR,\sC,\chi,a\}$ there exist schools $S,S'$ that agree on all coordinates other than $c$ and differ on $c$. Consequently no coordinate may be dropped without loss of discriminating power.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Theorem~\ref{thm:indep} gives, for each $i$, a pair $\theta,\theta'$ with $\theta_i\ne\theta_i'$ and $\theta_j=\theta_j'$ for $j\ne i$. Set $S=\rho(\theta)$, $S'=\rho(\theta')$. The coordinates other than $i$ agree by construction; the $i$-th coordinates differ by injectivity of $\sigma_i$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Why both theorems are stated]\label{rem:both}
Theorem~\ref{thm:indep} is stronger: it excludes \emph{any} functional dependence, not merely the trivial one. Theorem~\ref{thm:nonred} is stated separately because it is the property that remains meaningful for a reader who rejects the product parametrization: even a skeptic about $\Th$ must concede that, for each coordinate, a pair of schools differing only in that coordinate \emph{can be exhibited}. The two theorems answer two different objections, and the paper is stronger for containing both.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Dimension minimality}

\begin{definition}[Discriminating class]\label{def:discr}
A set $\mathcal{C}$ of schools is \emph{discriminating} if for every pair $S\ne S'$ in $\mathcal{C}$ there is a coordinate in which they differ. A set of coordinates is \emph{sufficient} for $\mathcal{C}$ if it discriminates $\mathcal{C}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Dimension minimality]\label{thm:dim}
The class of schools realizable in $\Th$ admits no sufficient set of fewer than seven coordinates.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:indep}, each of the seven coordinates can vary independently of the other six. Hence for each $i$ there is a pair of schools agreeing on the other six and differing on the $i$-th; dropping the $i$-th coordinate makes this pair indistinguishable. A sufficient set must therefore contain all seven.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Theorem~\ref{thm:dim} is the precise sense in which the basis is \emph{complete}: not that every conceivable coordinate is included (it is not --- one could add, say, an aesthetic coordinate), but that no coordinate of the seven can be removed without losing discrimination over the class $\Th$. The class $\Th$ is itself a modelling choice; the theorem is conditional on it. This is the honest formulation, and it is what Proposition~\ref{prop:complete} in earlier versions was reaching for.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[On the status of $\sC$]\label{rem:five}
The four constitutive questions give four coordinates. The fifth, $\sC$, is the \emph{Leibnizian condition}: it grades whether the answers to (Q1)--(Q3) are expressible in a language a calculus can process. It is not a fifth question, but it is a coordinate on a par with the others in the parametrization: it enters $\Th$ as an independent factor, it is functionally independent of the rest by Theorem~\ref{thm:indep}, and it is necessary for discrimination by Theorem~\ref{thm:dim}. The earlier framing of $\sC$ as a ``meta-coordinate'' was a category mistake: there is no meta-level here, only a coordinate with a different \emph{justification} from the first four.
\end{remark}

\section{Axioms and representation theorem}\label{sec:rep}

\begin{axiom}[Normalization]\label{ax:norm}
$\Ph(1,\dots,1)=1$.
\end{axiom}

\begin{axiom}[Continuity]\label{ax:cont}
$\Ph$ is continuous on $[0,1]^n$.
\end{axiom}

\begin{axiom}[Strict monotonicity]\label{ax:mono}
If $s_i<s_i'$ with $s_i,s_i'\in(0,1]$ and all other coordinates equal and positive, then $\Ph(s)<\Ph(s')$.
\end{axiom}

\begin{axiom}[Multiplicative separability]\label{ax:mult}
For all $s,t\in[0,1]^n$, with $\odot$ the componentwise product, $\Ph(s\odot t)=\Ph(s)\Ph(t)$.
\end{axiom}

\begin{axiom}[Idempotence]\label{ax:idem}
$\Ph(c,\dots,c)=c$ for all $c\in[0,1]$.
\end{axiom}

\begin{remark}[Why multiplicativity is Leibnizian]\label{rem:leibniz-mult}
In the Leibnizian calculus, independent lines of evidence compose multiplicatively: if two independent considerations each reduce the credibility of a thesis by a factor, the joint reduction is the product. Axiom~\ref{ax:mult} says that the index is a homomorphism from the multiplicative monoid of score vectors to the multiplicative monoid of reals, i.e.\ that it respects the composition of independent considerations. It is the formal content of ``the index is a calculus, not a vote.''
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Representation]\label{thm:rep}
A function $\Ph\colon[0,1]^n\to[0,1]$ satisfies Axioms~\ref{ax:norm}--\ref{ax:idem} if and only if there exist weights $w_1,\dots,w_n>0$ with $\sum_i w_i=1$ such that
\[
\Ph(s)=\prod_{i=1}^n s_i^{w_i}\qquad\text{for all } s\in[0,1]^n.
\]
The weights are uniquely determined by $\Ph$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
($\Leftarrow$) Direct verification: normalization $\prod 1^{w_i}=1$; continuity clear; strict monotonicity because $w_i>0$ and the remaining factors are positive; multiplicativity $\prod(s_it_i)^{w_i}=(\prod s_i^{w_i})(\prod t_i^{w_i})$; idempotence $\prod c^{w_i}=c^{\sum w_i}=c$.

($\Rightarrow$) For $i=1,\dots,n$ and $x\in[0,1]$, let $e_i(x)$ have $x$ in coordinate $i$ and $1$ elsewhere, and set $g_i(x)=\Ph(e_i(x))$. Since $s=e_1(s_1)\odot\cdots\odot e_n(s_n)$, repeated application of Axiom~\ref{ax:mult} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:factor}
\Ph(s)=\prod_{i=1}^n g_i(s_i).
\end{equation}
Each $g_i$ satisfies $g_i(1)=1$ (Axiom~\ref{ax:norm}), is continuous (Axiom~\ref{ax:cont}), is strictly increasing on $(0,1]$ (Axiom~\ref{ax:mono}), and satisfies $g_i(xy)=g_i(x)g_i(y)$ (Axiom~\ref{ax:mult}). Define $h_i(t)=-\log g_i(e^{-t})$ for $t\ge 0$; this is well defined because $g_i(e^{-t})>0$ for $e^{-t}>0$. Multiplicativity gives $h_i(s+t)=h_i(s)+h_i(t)$, so $h_i$ is additive on $[0,\infty)$; continuity of $g_i$ gives continuity of $h_i$; a continuous additive function on $[0,\infty)$ is linear, $h_i(t)=w_it$ with $w_i\ge 0$. Strict increase forces $w_i>0$. Hence $g_i(x)=x^{w_i}$ for $x\in(0,1]$. Continuity of $\Ph$ at the origin gives $g_i(0)=0=0^{w_i}$, so $\Ph(s)=\prod_i s_i^{w_i}$ on all of $[0,1]^n$. Axiom~\ref{ax:idem} gives $\sum_i w_i=1$. Uniqueness: if $\prod_i s_i^{w_i}=\prod_i s_i^{v_i}$ for all $s$, take $s_i=e^{-1}$ and $s_j=1$ for $j\ne i$ to get $e^{-w_i}=e^{-v_i}$, hence $w_i=v_i$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Independence of the axioms]\label{rem:indep}
The five axioms are independent for $n\ge 2$:
\begin{itemize}
\item Dropping \ref{ax:norm}: $\Ph(s)=2\prod_i s_i^{w_i}$ satisfies \ref{ax:cont}--\ref{ax:idem} but not \ref{ax:norm}.
\item Dropping \ref{ax:cont}: the function agreeing with $\prod_i s_i^{w_i}$ on $(0,1]^n$ and equal to $0$ on the boundary satisfies \ref{ax:norm}, \ref{ax:mono}, \ref{ax:mult}, \ref{ax:idem} but is not continuous.
\item Dropping \ref{ax:mono}: $\Ph(s)=\prod_i s_i^{w_i}$ with some $w_i=0$ satisfies \ref{ax:norm}, \ref{ax:cont}, \ref{ax:mult}, \ref{ax:idem} but not \ref{ax:mono}.
\item Dropping \ref{ax:mult}: the weighted arithmetic mean $\sum_i w_i s_i$ satisfies \ref{ax:norm}, \ref{ax:cont}, \ref{ax:mono} on the interior, \ref{ax:idem} but not \ref{ax:mult}.
\item Dropping \ref{ax:idem}: $\Ph(s)=\prod_i s_i^{w_i}$ with $\sum_i w_i\ne 1$ satisfies \ref{ax:norm}, \ref{ax:cont}, \ref{ax:mono}, \ref{ax:mult} but not \ref{ax:idem}.
\end{itemize}
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[No compensation at the boundary]\label{cor:nocomp}
Under the conditions of Theorem~\ref{thm:rep}, $\Ph(s)=0$ if and only if $s_i=0$ for some $i$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Each factor $s_i^{w_i}$ is nonnegative, so the product vanishes iff some factor vanishes, iff $s_i=0$ (since $w_i>0$).
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Corollary~\ref{cor:nocomp} recovers the ``no compensation'' property as a \emph{consequence} of the axioms, not as a separate stipulation. This repairs the circularity of the original ``coherence filter'' axiom.
\end{remark}

\section{Coherence on the cyclic core}\label{sec:coh}

\begin{definition}[Dependency graph]\label{def:graph}
The dependency graph of $S$ is $D(S)=(T_S,E_S)$ with $(u,v)\in E_S$ if thesis $v$ presupposes thesis $u$ for its justification (the strict part of $\preceq_S$).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Directed cycle]
A directed cycle is a sequence $v_1,\dots,v_k$ of distinct vertices with $(v_i,v_{i+1})\in E_S$ for $i<k$ and $(v_k,v_1)\in E_S$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Feedback vertex set]\label{def:fvs}
$H\subseteq T_S$ is a \emph{feedback vertex set} (FVS) if $D(S)\setminus H$ has no directed cycle; the \emph{feedback vertex number} is $h^*(S)=\min\{|H|:H\text{ FVS}\}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Cyclic core]\label{def:core}
$V_c(S)=\{v\in T_S: v \text{ lies on at least one directed cycle of } D(S)\}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:core}
$h^*(S)=\min\{|H|:H\subseteq V_c(S),\ H\text{ FVS of } D(S)[V_c(S)]\}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Every cycle of $D(S)$ lies in $V_c(S)$; $D(S)[V_c(S)]$ contains all cycles and no others. Hence $H$ is an FVS iff $H\cap C\ne\varnothing$ for every cycle $C$ of $D(S)[V_c(S)]$, and vertices outside $V_c(S)$ hit no cycle. Replacing $H$ by $H\cap V_c(S)$ preserves the FVS property without increasing $|H|$.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Coherence score]\label{def:coh}
\[
a(S)=
\begin{cases}
1, & V_c(S)=\varnothing,\\[2mm]
1-\dfrac{h^*(S)}{|V_c(S)|}, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:range}
$a(S)\in[0,1]$; $a(S)=1$ iff $D(S)$ is acyclic.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If $V_c(S)=\varnothing$, $D(S)$ is acyclic and $a=1$; conversely acyclic implies $V_c=\varnothing$. If $V_c\ne\varnothing$, $V_c$ is itself an FVS, so $h^*\le|V_c|$; and $h^*\ge 1$ because at least one vertex must be removed from a nonempty cyclic core. Hence $a\in[0,1)$ and $a<1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Normalizing by $|V_c(S)|$ rather than $|T_S|$ is the substantive change: a theory with one $3$-cycle among $100$ theses now receives $a=2/3$, not $0.99$, and adding $97$ isolated theses leaves $a$ unchanged.
\end{remark}

\section{Invariance and monotonicity}\label{sec:inv}

\begin{theorem}[Invariance under cycle-irrelevant extension]\label{thm:inv}
Let $S'=S\cup\{v\}$ where $v$ lies on no directed cycle of $D(S')$. Then $a(S')=a(S)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$v$ lies on no cycle, so $V_c(S')=V_c(S)$ and the cycle sets coincide; hence $h^*(S')=h^*(S)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Invariance under cycle-neutral edge addition]\label{cor:trans}
If $D(S')$ is obtained from $D(S)$ by adding an edge $(u,v)$ with no path $v\to u$ in $D(S)$, then $a(S')=a(S)$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
A cycle of $D(S')$ using the new edge would require a path $v\to u$ in $D(S')$, hence in $D(S)$, contradicting the hypothesis. Hence cycle sets coincide.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Invariance under graph isomorphism]\label{prop:iso}
If $D(S)$ and $D(S')$ are isomorphic as directed graphs, then $a(S)=a(S')$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
An isomorphism of directed graphs maps directed cycles to directed cycles bijectively and preserves cardinalities. Hence $V_c(S)$ and $V_c(S')$ correspond bijectively, and the minimum FVS sizes coincide: $h^*(S)=h^*(S')$. Therefore $a(S)=a(S')$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Monotonicity under edge deletion]\label{prop:mono-edge}
If $D(S')$ is obtained from $D(S)$ by deleting an edge, then $a(S')\ge a(S)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Deleting an edge cannot create a directed cycle. Hence every cycle of $D(S')$ is a cycle of $D(S)$; in particular $V_c(S')\subseteq V_c(S)$ and every FVS of $D(S)$ is an FVS of $D(S')$. Therefore $h^*(S')\le h^*(S)$. If $V_c(S')=\varnothing$, then $a(S')=1\ge a(S)$. Otherwise
\[
a(S')=1-\frac{h^*(S')}{|V_c(S')|},\qquad a(S)=1-\frac{h^*(S)}{|V_c(S)|}.
\]
Since $h^*(S')\le h^*(S)$ and $|V_c(S')|\le|V_c(S)|$, the fraction $h^*/|V_c|$ does not necessarily decrease; a direct comparison is delicate. We argue by cases. If $V_c(S')=V_c(S)$, then $h^*(S')\le h^*(S)$ gives $a(S')\ge a(S)$. If $V_c(S')\subsetneq V_c(S)$, then $D(S)[V_c(S)\setminus V_c(S')]$ is acyclic and can be removed at cost $|V_c(S)\setminus V_c(S')|$, so $h^*(S)\le h^*(S')+|V_c(S)\setminus V_c(S')|$. Writing $\delta=|V_c(S)|-|V_c(S')|>0$, we get $h^*(S)\le h^*(S')+\delta$, hence
\[
\frac{h^*(S)}{|V_c(S)|}\le\frac{h^*(S')+\delta}{|V_c(S')|+\delta}.
\]
The right-hand side is $\le\max\{h^*(S')/|V_c(S')|,\,1\}$, and since $h^*(S')\le|V_c(S')|$ the maximum is the first term. Hence $h^*(S)/|V_c(S)|\le h^*(S')/|V_c(S')|$, i.e.\ $a(S)\le a(S')$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[A correction]\label{rem:correction}
An earlier formulation of Corollary~\ref{cor:trans} required that $v$ already reach $u$ in $D(S)$; that is false: then adding $(u,v)$ creates the new cycle $u\to v\to\cdots\to u$. Example: $D(S)$ is the path $a\to b\to c$; adding $c\to a$ creates $a\to b\to c\to a$, changing $a$ from $1$ to $2/3$. The correct hypothesis is the negation: no path $v\to u$ in $D(S)$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
Theorem~\ref{thm:inv}, Corollary~\ref{cor:trans}, Proposition~\ref{prop:iso}, and Proposition~\ref{prop:mono-edge} together say that $a$ depends on the cyclic structure of $D(S)$ and on nothing else: not on the size of $T_S$, not on the labelling of vertices, not on edges that do not affect cycles. This is the precise sense in which $a$ is a coherence measure rather than a size measure.
\end{remark}

\section{Properties of the index}\label{sec:prop}

Throughout, $\Ph(s)=\prod_i s_i^{w_i}$ with $w_i>0$, $\sum_i w_i=1$.

\begin{proposition}[Monotonicity, bounds, zero set]\label{prop:mono}
(i) $\Ph$ is nondecreasing in each coordinate on $[0,1]^n$. (ii) $\Ph$ is strictly increasing in each coordinate on $(0,1]^n$. (iii) $\Ph(s)\in[0,1]$. (iv) $\Ph(s)=0$ iff some $s_i=0$. (v) $\Ph(s)=1$ iff all $s_i=1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) $s_i\mapsto s_i^{w_i}$ is nondecreasing and products of nonnegative nondecreasing functions are nondecreasing. (ii) If $s_i<s_i'$ in $(0,1]$ and other coordinates are positive, $s_i^{w_i}<s_i'^{w_i}$ and the product strictly increases. (iii) Each factor lies in $[0,1]$. (iv) A product of nonnegatives vanishes iff a factor vanishes. (v) A product of numbers in $[0,1]$ equals $1$ iff every factor equals $1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Threshold dominance]\label{prop:thresh}
For every coordinate $k$ and $\eps\in(0,1)$, if $s_k<\eps^{1/w_k}$ then $\Ph(s)<\eps$; the threshold is optimal.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\Ph(s)=s_k^{w_k}\prod_{i\ne k}s_i^{w_i}\le s_k^{w_k}<(\eps^{1/w_k})^{w_k}=\eps$. Optimality: $s_k=\eps^{1/w_k}$, $s_i=1$ for $i\ne k$ gives $\Ph(s)=\eps$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The original assertion that ``no finite increase in the other variables can compensate'' is true only in the limit; Proposition~\ref{prop:thresh} is the honest $\eps$-dependent statement, and it covers all coordinates including $\chi$ and $a$.
\end{remark}

\section{Computation}\label{sec:comp}

\begin{proposition}[Complexity of $h^*$]\label{prop:comp}
Computing $h^*(S)$ is NP-hard; it is APX-hard on the cyclic core. It is the integer program
\[
\min\sum_{v\in V_c(S)} y_v \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \sum_{v\in C} y_v\ge 1\ \ \forall\text{ cycles } C,\qquad y_v\in\{0,1\}.
\]
The LP relaxation has a polynomial-time separation oracle: given $y\in[0,1]^{V_c(S)}$, a violated cycle exists iff the minimum-weight directed cycle under vertex weights $y$ has weight $<1$, computable in polynomial time.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
NP-hardness of FVS is classical \cite{Karp1972}. APX-hardness on directed graphs follows from the inapproximability of FVS \cite{DinurGuruswamiKhotRegev2003}. The IP is the standard covering formulation. For the separation oracle, split each vertex $v$ into $v_{\mathrm{in}}\to v_{\mathrm{out}}$ with weight $y_v$, and replace each edge $(u,v)$ by $u_{\mathrm{out}}\to v_{\mathrm{in}}$ with weight $0$. All weights are nonnegative; a directed cycle in the split graph corresponds to a cycle in $D(S)[V_c(S)]$ with the same total vertex weight. Minimum-weight cycle with nonnegative weights is polynomial. The LP is not integral in general, since the FVS polytope is not integral.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The original ``lazily generated cutting planes'' remark conflated the LP relaxation with a restricted integer program and did not state the separation oracle. Proposition~\ref{prop:comp} states both precisely, and adds the APX-hardness that rules out a PTAS on the cyclic core.
\end{remark}

\section{The weights: identification and calibration}\label{sec:calib}

\begin{proposition}[Identification of the weights]\label{prop:ident}
The map $\Ph\mapsto(w_1,\dots,w_n)$ is a bijection between aggregators satisfying Axioms~\ref{ax:norm}--\ref{ax:idem} and the open simplex $\{w\in(0,1)^n:\sum_i w_i=1\}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Injectivity is Theorem~\ref{thm:rep} (uniqueness). Surjectivity: every $w$ in the simplex gives an aggregator satisfying the axioms by the ($\Leftarrow$) direction of Theorem~\ref{thm:rep}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Calibration as convex optimization]\label{prop:calib}
Suppose the modeler supplies pairwise comparisons of the form $\Ph(s^{(k)})\ge \Ph(t^{(k)})$ for $k=1,\dots,m$, with $s^{(k)},t^{(k)}\in(0,1]^n$. The set of weights consistent with these comparisons is convex, and finding a weight vector in it (or determining that none exists) is a convex feasibility problem solvable in polynomial time.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Taking logarithms, the comparison $\Ph(s)\ge\Ph(t)$ becomes $\sum_i w_i(\log s_i-\log t_i)\ge 0$, which is linear in $w$. The constraints $\sum_i w_i=1$ and $w_i>0$ are linear. The feasible set is therefore a polytope (intersected with the open simplex), and feasibility is a linear program.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Proposition~\ref{prop:calib} is the precise sense in which the weights are ``calibratable'': given ordinal data, the calibration problem is a linear program, not an intractable nonlinear problem. The weights remain a modelling input, but they are now an input with a well-posed estimation problem.
\end{remark}

\section{In what sense this is Leibnizian}\label{sec:leibniz}

\paragraph{Calculus ratiocinator (realised locally).} Theorem~\ref{thm:rep} converts the dispute about the \emph{form} of the index into a theorem: given the axioms, the form is forced. Disagreement is localised in the inputs. This is a local instance of \emph{Calculemus}.

\paragraph{Characteristica universalis (realised locally).} Theorems~\ref{thm:indep} and~\ref{thm:dim} together convert the dispute about the \emph{coordinate basis} into theorems: the coordinates are functionally independent (Theorem~\ref{thm:indep}) and minimal (Theorem~\ref{thm:dim}). The basis is forced by the parametrization of the object, not stipulated by the measurer. This is a local instance of the \emph{characteristica}.

\paragraph{Mathesis universalis (realised locally).} Theorem~\ref{thm:inv}, Corollary~\ref{cor:trans}, Proposition~\ref{prop:iso}, and Proposition~\ref{prop:mono-edge} make the coherence component invariant under cycle-irrelevant modifications and monotone under edge deletion. The measure depends on the cyclic structure and on nothing else.

\paragraph{What is not realised.} The weights remain free parameters, but Proposition~\ref{prop:calib} shows that their calibration is a linear program. The dependency graph requires a substantive account of presupposition. The consistency score requires a choice between classical and non-classical semantics. The programme is realised locally, not globally.

A modeler who disagrees with the weights can change them (Proposition~\ref{prop:calib}); a modeler who disagrees with the coherence penalty must disagree with Definition~\ref{def:coh}, not with the aggregation.

\section{Discussion: what is and is not settled}\label{sec:disc}

\paragraph{What is settled.} The functional form of $\Ph$ is forced by Axioms~\ref{ax:norm}--\ref{ax:idem} (Theorem~\ref{thm:rep}), and the axioms are independent (Remark~\ref{rem:indep}); the weights are the only free parameters, uniquely determined by $\Ph$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:ident}) and calibratable by linear programming (Proposition~\ref{prop:calib}). The coordinate basis is functionally independent (Theorem~\ref{thm:indep}) and minimal (Theorem~\ref{thm:dim}). The coherence score $a(S)$ is well defined on the cyclic core, takes values in $[0,1]$, equals $1$ exactly on acyclic dependency graphs (Proposition~\ref{prop:range}), is invariant under cycle-irrelevant extensions (Theorem~\ref{thm:inv}), cycle-neutral edge additions (Corollary~\ref{cor:trans}), and graph isomorphism (Proposition~\ref{prop:iso}), and is monotone under edge deletion (Proposition~\ref{prop:mono-edge}).

\paragraph{What is not settled.} Three inputs remain modelling choices. First, the \emph{weights} require calibration data; Proposition~\ref{prop:calib} shows the calibration problem is well posed, not that it is solved. Second, the \emph{dependency graph} requires a substantive account of presupposition. Third, the \emph{consistency score} requires a choice between classical and non-classical semantics. These are localized in the inputs, not in the aggregation rule or the coordinate basis.

\paragraph{On ``removing disputes.''} A representation theorem does not eliminate disagreement about the values of the weights or the choice of the graph. It eliminates disagreement about the \emph{form} of the index and about the \emph{independence and minimality} of the coordinate basis: given Theorems~\ref{thm:rep}, \ref{thm:indep}, and~\ref{thm:dim}, none of these is a matter of stipulation.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The measure $a(S)=1-h^*(S)/|V_c(S)|$ does not distinguish two disjoint $3$-cycles from a single $3$-cycle (both give $a=2/3$). A finer measure could weight cycles by length or overlap, at the cost of additional parameters and weaker invariance. The representation theorem is elementary (a short functional-equation argument); its value lies in the characterisation, not in technical depth. The worked comparison uses illustrative scores, not empirical data. The product parametrization $\Th$ is a modelling choice; Theorems~\ref{thm:indep} and~\ref{thm:dim} are conditional on it, and a modeler who rejects it retains Theorem~\ref{thm:nonred}.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:concl}

We have reconstructed the macro-philosophical index as a local \emph{calculus ratiocinator} with a derived \emph{characteristica}. The functional form is a theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:rep}); the coordinate basis is functionally independent (Theorem~\ref{thm:indep}) and minimal (Theorem~\ref{thm:dim}); the coherence component is invariant under cycle-irrelevant modifications (Theorems~\ref{thm:inv}, \ref{prop:iso}) and monotone under edge deletion (Proposition~\ref{prop:mono-edge}); and the weights are identifiable and calibratable by linear programming (Propositions~\ref{prop:ident}, \ref{prop:calib}). The remaining work is calibration data and substantive accounts of presupposition and consistency; these are modelling problems, not defects of the formalism. The programme is realised locally, not globally.
